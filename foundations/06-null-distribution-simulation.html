Null distributions: an interactive simulation

The null-hypothesis-testing notebook gave us one observed quality-control sample:

\[ n = 25, \qquad \bar{X}=20.20\text{ mm}, \qquad S=0.40\text{ mm}. \]

We want to know where that one observed sample mean would stand if the null claim (\(H_0: \mu = 20.00\ \text{mm}\)) were true. Choose a null population below, then generate many hypothetical samples of 25 shafts. The resulting distribution of sample means is the null distribution for this test.

The Normal and Uniform models have different shapes for individual shaft diameters, but their displayed parameters give both models the same null mean and population SD. The simulation does not estimate these parameters; it takes them as the specified null model.

TipWhat to watch

The dashed line marks the null mean, 20.00 mm, and the solid red line marks the observed mean, 20.20 mm. First build the null distribution and assess how unusual the observation looks. Then use Show rejection region to reveal the two-sided decision rule for a chosen \(\alpha\). The simulation estimates the two-sided p-value by counting sample means at least as extreme as the observation.

Observed sample: \(n = 25\); \(\bar{X} = 20.20\ \text{mm}\); \(S = 0.40\ \text{mm}\).

No trials yet. Add a small batch to begin.

With a Normal population, the sample mean is exactly Normal. With a Uniform population, the sample mean is only approximately Normal—but with 25 observations, the central limit theorem makes the two null distributions look similar.
